Week 4 · Burn Unit · Patient MRN-00452

Marcus Williams, 17 M

Chief complaint: "I was cooking and the grease from the pan splashed on my right arm. It hurts so bad and there are blisters everywhere."

Before you write: Read the entire vignette first. The highlighted clues tell you which SOAP section each piece of information belongs in.
Clinical Vignette — Burn Unit

Marcus Williams is a 17-year-old male who presents to the ED burn unit after a kitchen grease splash to the right forearm approximately 2 hours ago. He reports pain rated 8/10, constant, burning in character. He states he immediately ran cold water over the arm at home but has not taken any pain medication. He denies loss of sensation to the area.

On examination, vital signs are: T 98.6°F, BP 122/78 mmHg, HR 92 bpm, RR 18 breaths/min, O₂ sat 98% on room air. The right forearm shows erythema and edema extending approximately 8cm × 5cm over the dorsal surface. Multiple intact and ruptured blisters are present. The wound surface appears moist and glistening. Dermis is visible. No dry, leathery, or painless areas noted. Capillary refill is brisk bilaterally. Estimated TBSA involvement: approximately 2%.

What you are being asked to do: Using this clinical vignette, write a complete SOAP note for Marcus's burn unit visit. You must use at least 8 Phase 2 terms correctly across the note. Click "2. Step-by-Step" above to begin building your note section by section.
Step-by-Step Guide · W4 Burn Case

Building Your SOAP Note

Click each section to open the guidance. Answer the questions in your own notes, then check the exemplar.

Remember: S = what the patient TELLS you. O = what you SEE and MEASURE. A = your clinical judgment. P = your plan of action.
S

Subjective — The Patient's Story

What the patient tells you in their own words

▶

The S section captures who the patient is, why they came in, and what they experienced — in their own words when possible.

  • Who is the patient? (age, sex) — start every S section with this
  • What is their chief complaint — ideally in quotes?
  • What was the mechanism of injury? How did it happen?
  • When did it happen? How long ago?
  • What symptoms are they reporting? Pain rating? Character of pain?
  • What have they tried at home before coming in?
  • Any relevant negatives they deny? (e.g., "denies loss of sensation")
From the vignette, your S section should include: 17-year-old male · kitchen grease splash mechanism · approximately 2 hours ago · pain 8/10, burning character · ran cold water at home · denies loss of sensation. Write the chief complaint in quotation marks.
O

Objective — What You Find

Numbers, measurements, physical findings — no patient opinions

▶

The O section is all facts. Vital signs with units. Physical exam findings using precise clinical terms. This is where most of your Phase 2 vocabulary lives.

  • What are the vital signs? (T, BP, HR, RR, O₂ — must include units)
  • Where exactly is the wound? (location, size in cm × cm)
  • What does the wound LOOK like? Describe color, texture, moisture
  • Are blisters present? Intact or ruptured?
  • What layer of skin is involved? Can you see dermis?
  • Is there any dry, leathery, or painless tissue? (This rules in/out 3rd degree)
  • What is the estimated TBSA?
Clinical term list for this O section: erythema (redness), edema (swelling), dermis (middle skin layer), blisters/vesicles, TBSA (total body surface area), ecchymosis (bruising — check if present), eschar (leathery crust — NOT present here, which is important). Use these terms instead of everyday words.
A

Assessment — Your Clinical Judgment

What is your diagnosis? What degree burn is this?

▶

The A section is your clinical conclusion based on what you observed. For a burn case, you must classify the burn and justify your classification.

  • What degree burn is this? (1st, 2nd partial, 2nd full, 3rd, 4th)
  • What are the key features that tell you the degree? (name them)
  • What degree is RULED OUT and why? (e.g., no eschar = rules out 3rd)
  • What is the estimated TBSA involvement?
  • Is this a minor, moderate, or major burn by clinical criteria?
Decision logic: Blisters + moist/glistening + dermis visible + pain 8/10 = 2nd degree partial thickness burn. The pain rules out 3rd degree (nerve endings destroyed = painless). No eschar confirms no full thickness involvement. Write "2nd degree (partial thickness) burn" in your A — never just "burn."
P

Plan — What Happens Next

Immediate treatment, wound care, follow-up, patient education

▶

The P section is your action list. For a burn case, think in layers: immediate wound care, pain management, infection prevention, and follow-up.

  • What immediate wound care is needed? (irrigation, debridement, dressing)
  • What about pain management?
  • How do you prevent infection? (topical antibiotics, dressing type)
  • Does this patient need admission, or can they go home?
  • When should they follow up?
  • What should you teach the patient before they leave?
Clinical terms for the P section: debridement (removing dead tissue), granulation (new tissue — expected in healing), wound irrigation, topical antibiotic, non-adherent dressing, outpatient wound care, return precautions. A strong Plan has at least 4 numbered items.

Patient: Marcus Williams · MRN-00452 · 17 M · Burn Unit

S Subjective ▶

17-year-old male presents to the burn unit with chief complaint: "I was cooking and the grease from the pan splashed on my right arm. It hurts so bad and there are blisters everywhere." Why this S earns full credit: Patient age and sex appear in the first line. Chief complaint is in direct quotes. Mechanism is specific — thermal injury from grease splash — not just "he got burned." Pain is rated AND described in character. Home care is documented. Relevant negatives are included ("denies loss of sensation" — this matters because sensation is part of burn depth assessment).

Vital Signs: T 98.6°F oral · BP 122/78 mmHg · HR 92 bpm · RR 18 breaths/min · O₂ sat 98% on room air · Pain 8/10

Why this O earns full credit: Every vital sign has its unit. The wound is described with exact dimensions (cm × cm). Each clinical finding uses precise language: erythema not "redness," edema not "swelling," dermis not "deep layer." Critically, the note says "No eschar identified" — documenting what is ABSENT is just as important as what is present, because it rules out 3rd degree.

2nd degree (partial thickness) burn, right dorsal forearm, approximately 2% TBSA. Classification supported by: blistering, moist wound surface, dermis visible, pain intact (8/10). Third-degree burn ruled out: no escharabsence of eschar = nerve endings intact = partial thickness only, no painless or leathery regions. Why this A earns full credit: The burn is classified precisely — not just "2nd degree" but "2nd degree partial thickness." The reasoning is stated explicitly: three signs that confirm it, one sign that rules out 3rd degree. A severity judgment is made (minor burn). This is how clinicians think — they don't just name the diagnosis, they justify it.
P Plan ▶
  1. Wound care: Gentle wound irrigation with sterile saline. Careful debridementdébrider = to remove dead/damaged tissue to promote healing of ruptured blister material. Apply topical antibiotic (silver sulfadiazine). Non-adherent dressing applied and secured.
  2. Pain management: Ibuprofen 400mg PO q6h PRN. Reassess pain following initial dressing application.
  3. Discharge with outpatient wound care instructions: Daily dressing changes. Expect granulationgranulum = little grain · new beefy-red tissue filling the wound bed during healing tissue to begin forming in 3–5 days.
  4. Follow-up: Return to burn clinic in 3–5 days for wound reassessment. Return to ED sooner if worsening pain, fever >101°F, or signs of infection develop.
  5. Patient education: Reviewed burn wound care, dressing change technique, and infection warning signs with patient and parent.
Why this P earns full credit: Five numbered action items. Each uses clinical language — debridement, purulence, erythema, granulation. The plan is sequenced logically: treat → medicate → discharge instructions → follow-up → education. The patient isn't just told "come back if it gets worse" — specific warning signs are named.
Week 4 · Key Terms for SOAP Writing

Terms You Need in This Note

These are the Phase 2 terms most likely to appear in a burn-related SOAP note on the Boards exam.

erythema
Greek: erythros (red)
Redness from vasodilation. Use instead of "redness."
Use in O: "erythema extending 3cm beyond wound margins"
dermis
derm/o (skin)
Middle skin layer — contains blood vessels and nerve endings.
Use in O: "dermis visible through wound bed"
eschar
Greek: eschara (scab)
Dry, dark, leathery dead tissue. ABSENCE of eschar rules out 3rd degree.
Use in O or A: "no eschar identified — 3rd degree excluded"
TBSA
Total Body Surface Area
Percentage of body surface burned. Calculated with Rule of Nines.
Use in O and A: "estimated 2% TBSA involvement"
debridement
French: débrider
Removal of dead, damaged, or infected tissue to promote healing.
Use in P: "gentle debridement of ruptured blister material"
granulation
Latin: granulum (little grain)
New connective tissue forming during wound healing. Beefy-red appearance.
Use in P: "granulation tissue expected in 3–5 days"
purulence
Latin: pus
Pus formation — sign of infection. Yellow-green discharge.
Use in P: "return if purulence develops"
edema
Greek: oidema (swelling)
Swelling from fluid accumulation in tissue. Use instead of "swelling."
Use in O: "edema noted over the dorsal forearm"
subcutaneous
sub- + cutane/o (skin)
Below the skin. 4th degree burns reach subcutaneous tissue and bone.
Use in A when ruling in/out deeper injury

Destiny Williams, 15 F

Chief complaint: "I fell off my bike trying to stop myself and my wrist has been killing me ever since."

Note: This is a different patient (Destiny Williams) — a new case so you practice independently, not copy from Marcus's chart.
Clinical Vignette — Orthopedic Trauma

Destiny Williams is a 15-year-old female who presents to the orthopedic ED after a fall from her bicycle, landing with outstretched hands (FOOSH mechanism) approximately 1 hour ago. She reports pain rated 9/10, sharp, worse with any movement. She denies any numbness or tingling initially but now reports mild tingling in her thumb and index finger.

Vital signs: T 98.2°F, BP 116/74, HR 98 bpm, RR 18, O₂ sat 99%. Right wrist examination reveals significant edema and ecchymosis over the distal radius. There is visible deformity with dorsal displacement of the distal fragment. Crepitus noted on initial assessment. X-ray reveals a fracture line that spirals around the shaft of the distal radius, with two major fragments. Skin is intact over the fracture site. Post-splint CMS assessment: cap refill 3 seconds, patient unable to fully extend fingers, tingling persists in thumb/index distribution.

What makes this case harder: You need to classify the fracture using two descriptors (type + open vs. closed). You also need to document the CMS findings accurately and recognize that the post-splint CMS shows two abnormalities — this is clinically urgent information that belongs in your Plan.
Step-by-Step Guide · W5 Fracture Case

Building Your SOAP Note

Fracture cases require extra precision in the O section — CMS findings and fracture classification are both tested.

Fracture rule: Always classify with TWO descriptors — the fracture pattern (spiral, transverse, etc.) AND whether it is open or closed.
S

Subjective

Patient story, mechanism, symptoms

▶
  • Who is the patient? Age and sex in the first sentence.
  • What is the mechanism? Be specific — FOOSH is a clinical abbreviation you should use.
  • What is their chief complaint in quotes?
  • Pain rating and character?
  • Any neurological symptoms? (tingling, numbness — these matter for fracture management)
Key for the S: The tingling in thumb and index finger is a neurological symptom that suggests possible nerve involvement. It belongs in S because the patient is reporting it. Later, in O, you confirm it with your CMS assessment.
O

Objective — Vitals, X-ray, CMS

This section must include your CMS findings table

▶
  • Vital signs with all units
  • Wound/fracture site description — location, deformity, swelling, bruising
  • X-ray findings — describe the fracture line, number of fragments, displacement
  • CMS CHECK — must document all 3 components: Circulation (cap refill), Motion (can they move fingers?), Sensation (any tingling/numbness?)
  • Is skin intact? (This determines open vs. closed classification)
CMS documentation format: Write it as three named items — "C: cap refill 3 seconds (delayed). M: patient unable to fully extend fingers. S: tingling in thumb and index finger distribution." This makes it clear and easy to grade. A 3-second cap refill is ABNORMAL (normal is under 2 seconds).
A

Assessment — Fracture Classification

Name the fracture type precisely — two descriptors required

▶
  • What type of fracture based on the X-ray description? (spiral, transverse, greenstick, comminuted, compression)
  • Is it open (compound) or closed? How do you know?
  • What bone? What region of the bone?
  • Is there displacement? How much?
  • What does the abnormal CMS tell you clinically?
Classification logic: "Fracture line spirals around the shaft" = spiral fracture. "Skin is intact" = closed fracture. Put it together: closed spiral fracture, distal radius, with dorsal displacement. Then add your CMS finding: "Post-splint CMS shows delayed capillary refill and incomplete motion — neurovascular compromise cannot be excluded."
P

Plan — Urgent CMS Response + Fracture Management

Abnormal CMS must be addressed FIRST in your plan

▶
  • The CMS is abnormal — what do you do immediately? (This is your first plan item)
  • What imaging is needed?
  • Does this fracture likely need ORIF or can it be managed conservatively?
  • What is the ortho consult plan?
  • What do you tell the patient and family?
CMS abnormality → immediate action: "Loosen and bivalve the splint immediately. Reassess CMS in 15 minutes." This MUST be your first plan item when CMS is compromised. Ignoring abnormal CMS findings in your Plan is the most dangerous mistake you can make on this exam.

  6. Patient/family education: Reviewed fracture findings, surgical possibility, and importance of reporting any changes in sensation or color to the nursing staff immediately.
Why this P earns full credit: The FIRST plan item addresses the abnormal CMS — this is the correct clinical priority. ORIF is named and justified. The plan is numbered, sequenced by urgency, and includes admission reasoning. Six items = thorough coverage.

Week 6 · Sports Medicine · Patient MRN-00891

Jordan Kim, 16 NB

Chief complaint: "My shoulder has been sore for weeks and now I can't raise my arm all the way. It happened during swim practice."

W6 SOAP notes are different: This is a sports medicine progress note — not an emergency case. The focus shifts to ROM deficit documentation, functional limitation, and rehab planning.
Clinical Vignette — Sports Medicine Clinic

Jordan Kim is a 16-year-old non-binary swimmer presenting to the sports medicine clinic for evaluation of right shoulder pain, 3 weeks duration. Pain began during swim practice with overhead freestyle stroke. Rates pain 4/10 at rest, 7/10 with overhead activity. Denies any acute traumatic event. Reports gradual onset with increasing stiffness.

Vital signs stable. Right shoulder exam: no edema or ecchymosis visible. Tenderness to palpation over the supraspinatus tendon insertion. Goniometric ROM assessment performed bilaterally using a standard goniometer:

Shoulder Abduction: Right AROM 110° / Right PROM 140° · Left AROM 178° / Left PROM 180°
Shoulder Flexion: Right AROM 120° / Right PROM 155° · Left AROM 175° / Left PROM 178°
Shoulder External Rotation: Right AROM 50° / Right PROM 65° · Left AROM 85° / Left PROM 88°

Pain reproduced with resisted shoulder abduction. Empty can test positive. Biceps tendon intact. Neurovascular status intact bilaterally.

What makes this case unique: You must document ROM using the correct format (AROM vs. PROM), calculate deficits by comparing to the contralateral side, and interpret what the AROM/PROM gap tells you clinically. The Plan for this case is a rehab protocol, not an emergency procedure.
Step-by-Step Guide · W6 ROM Case

Building Your SOAP Note

ROM cases require precise numbers and correct interpretation of the AROM vs. PROM gap.

AROM vs. PROM rule: If PROM > AROM, the joint CAN move (structure intact) but the patient CAN'T produce the motion actively — this means pain or muscle weakness, not a structural block.
S

Subjective

Include onset, duration, activity context, and bilateral comparison

▶
  • Who is the patient? Age, sex/gender identity.
  • How long has this been happening? Gradual or sudden?
  • What activity caused it? Be specific — overhead freestyle stroke.
  • Pain rating at rest AND with activity (this is different from trauma cases)
  • What makes it worse? What makes it better?
  • Any prior shoulder injuries?
Key for this S: Sports medicine SOAP notes capture the functional context — what activity provoked it, what movements are limited, how it affects their sport. Include "gradual onset" and "increasing stiffness" — these point toward overuse, not acute trauma.
O

Objective — ROM Table + Physical Findings

Document AROM and PROM separately, calculate the deficit

▶
  • Vital signs (stable for this case)
  • What does the shoulder look like? (No edema, no ecchymosis here — important to note)
  • Where is the tenderness? (Name the specific structure — supraspinatus tendon insertion)
  • ROM measurements — document as: Movement · AROM right/left · PROM right/left
  • What is the DEFICIT? (Compare right AROM to left AROM, and right PROM to left PROM)
  • What special tests were performed and what did they show?
How to calculate deficit: Abduction AROM: Right 110° vs. Left 178° = 68° deficit. Abduction PROM: Right 140° vs. Left 180° = 40° deficit. The gap between AROM (110°) and PROM (140°) = 30° — this tells you there's still structural mobility, but active muscle recruitment is limited by pain.
A

Assessment — Diagnosis + Clinical Interpretation

Name the condition and explain what the ROM findings mean

▶
  • What structure is likely injured? (Supraspinatus — part of the rotator cuff)
  • What is the clinical diagnosis? (Supraspinatus tendinitis / rotator cuff tendinopathy)
  • What does the AROM/PROM gap tell you about the nature of the limitation?
  • What does "positive empty can test" confirm?
Assessment logic: "Right supraspinatus tendinitis (rotator cuff tendinopathy) — overuse pattern consistent with repetitive overhead swimming. PROM exceeds AROM at all planes, indicating pain-limited active range rather than structural joint block. Supraspinatus involvement confirmed by positive empty can test and pain with resisted abduction."
P

Plan — Rehab Protocol + Activity Modification

This plan is a rehab plan, not an emergency plan

▶
  • What activity modifications are needed? (Modify swim training — no overhead work)
  • What PT/rehab interventions? (Eccentric strengthening, AROM exercises)
  • Pain management? (NSAIDs, ice)
  • When do they follow up? What are the return-to-sport criteria?
  • What do you warn them about? (Not pushing through pain)
Rehab plan language: Use "AROM exercises," "eccentric strengthening protocol," "activity modification," "return-to-sport criteria," "NSAIDs," and "follow-up in 4–6 weeks." Avoid emergency language (no IV lines, no ORIF, no admission) — this is an outpatient sports medicine visit.

Common SOAP Note Mistakes
Side-by-side comparisons of what novice writers do vs. what clinical writers do. Study these before the exam.

Why these mistakes happen

You are not a clinician yet — and that's fine. Most SOAP note errors come from three habits: using everyday language instead of medical terms, putting information in the wrong section, and writing vague plans. This tab shows you the exact patterns to avoid.

Mistake #1 — Wrong Section

Putting objective findings in the Subjective section

❌ Novice writer

"S: The patient came in with blisters and redness on his arm and his blood pressure was 122/78."

✓ Clinical writer

"S: 17-year-old male presents with chief complaint: 'There are blisters everywhere and it burns.' Mechanism: grease splash to right forearm. Pain 8/10."

"O: BP 122/78 mmHg. Erythema and blistering noted over dorsal right forearm."

Rule: S = only what the patient SAYS. O = only what you SEE or MEASURE. Blood pressure is a measurement — it belongs in O. Blisters are a physical finding — also O. The patient's own words go in S.
Mistake #2 — Plain English

Writing everyday words where clinical terms are required

❌ Novice writer

"O: The wrist is really swollen and bruised. He can't move his fingers and his fingers feel a little numb."

✓ Clinical writer

"O: Significant edema and ecchymosis over the distal radius. CMS: C — cap refill 3 sec (delayed). M — patient unable to fully extend digits. S — paresthesia in thumb and index finger distribution."

Plain English earns partial credit but caps your score at a maximum of 14/20. Replace: "swollen" → edema · "bruised" → ecchymosis · "numb" → paresthesia · "can't move fingers" → "motion deficient — unable to extend digits."
Mistake #3 — Missing CMS Format

Writing "CMS intact" without documenting the three components

❌ Novice writer

"O: CMS was checked and it was abnormal."

✓ Clinical writer

"O: Post-splint CMS: C — capillary refill 3 seconds (delayed, normal <2 sec). M — patient unable to fully extend fingers. S — tingling in thumb and index finger (median nerve distribution)."

CMS must always be documented as three named components with specific findings. "CMS abnormal" earns 0 points. You must state what is abnormal about each letter — C, M, and S — separately.
Mistake #4 — Vague Assessment

Naming the body part instead of the clinical diagnosis

❌ Novice writer

"A: The patient has a broken wrist."

"A: 2nd degree burn."

✓ Clinical writer

"A: Closed spiral fracture, distal radius, with dorsal displacement. Post-splint CMS compromised — neurovascular concern."

"A: 2nd degree (partial thickness) burn, right dorsal forearm, ~2% TBSA. 3rd degree excluded — no eschar, pain intact."

The A section requires: diagnosis name + location + qualifier + your reasoning. Two words is never enough. The examiner wants to see that you can classify, locate, and justify — not just name.
Mistake #5 — Plan Without Priorities

Listing generic steps without addressing the most urgent finding first

❌ Novice writer

"P: 1. Give pain medicine. 2. Splint the wrist. 3. Tell patient to come back if it gets worse."

✓ Clinical writer

"P: 1. URGENT — bivalve splint immediately — CMS compromised. Reassess in 15 min. 2. CT wrist for surgical planning. 3. ORIF likely — ortho consult placed. 4. IV morphine for pain. 5. NPO — pre-op pending."

The Plan must be prioritized. If something is urgent — abnormal CMS, high TBSA burn, open fracture — it goes FIRST. A plan that addresses pain before a neurovascular emergency would be considered clinically unsafe.
Mistake #6 — Wrong AROM/PROM Interpretation

Saying PROM > AROM means the joint is "stuck"

❌ Novice writer

"A: Patient has limited motion in the shoulder and cannot move it properly. The joint is stiff."

✓ Clinical writer

"A: PROM exceeds AROM at all planes (e.g., abduction PROM 140° vs. AROM 110°) — indicates pain-limited active range, not structural joint block or contracture. Passive motion is nearly preserved, confirming the joint can move when the patient's own muscle inhibition is removed."

PROM > AROM = muscle/pain problem. If BOTH were severely limited, that would suggest a structural block or contracture. The gap between them is the clinically important finding — not just the numbers themselves.
What Earns Full Credit

The 8-term minimum — used correctly, not just listed

Using 8 terms means using them in clinically correct sentences — not listing them at the bottom of the note. "Terms used: erythema, edema, dermis..." earns 0 points. The terms must appear naturally in your S, O, A, and P sections, applied to the actual clinical findings.

Exam-Day Reminder

60 minutes total — Section D (SOAP note) = 10 minutes

Budget your time. Spend 2 minutes reading the vignette carefully. Write S and O first — they are faster (direct from the case). Then A and P. If you're stuck on A, write your best assessment and move to P — don't leave P blank. An incomplete P is worth more than a perfect A with nothing after it.
